Case study

Designing awareness for hybrid teams

How do you carry the fact that a colleague is around — and reachable — without building one more thing that interrupts them?

Role
Sole designer and researcher
Duration
2019 – 2026
Context
Doctoral research, TU/e Industrial Design
Output
Design framework, three prototypes, two studies

The problem

When a team splits between home and office, the cheap information disappears first. Meetings survive — they are scheduled, they have owners. What goes is everything that used to cost nothing: knowing whether someone is at their desk, whether they are deep in something, whether this is a reasonable moment to ask a two-minute question.

Teams replaced that with status dots and calendar blocks. Both fail in the same way. A green dot tells you someone's client is running; it tells you nothing about whether they can be interrupted. So people either ignore the signal or misread it, and the small exchanges that hold a team together stop happening.

I spent six years on one question: can a system carry that information without becoming another thing demanding attention?

Framing: awareness is built, not delivered

The first move was a reframing that changed everything downstream. Most systems treat awareness as a delivery problem — get the right status to the right person. But people don't receive awareness, they construct it, continuously and mostly without noticing, from peripheral cues in a shared space.

That shifts the design question from is the information delivered to what is it like to build an understanding from it. I called the target human-like experiences: information that behaves the way presence behaves in a shared room, rather than information formatted as a notification.

Building a design space

A framing is not actionable on its own. I built a design space from an annotated portfolio of existing and speculative systems, then ran co-design workshops where participants generated new concepts inside the emerging structure.

The space is organised around four aspects:

  • Social cues as information carriers — using movement, posture and gesture rather than symbols.
  • Processing information with ambiguity — deliberately deciding how precisely to say something.
  • Delivering information in ambient design — placing it at the periphery rather than in the foreground.
  • Built-in lightweight interactions — letting people respond without it becoming a task.

A designer can enter from any aspect and reason through the others. It works as a generative tool for ideation and as an analytic lens for reading systems that already exist. Published at ACM DIS 2024.

Three concepts, one variable

I designed three displays that carry a colleague's presence through movement rather than an icon. Each one takes a different position on a single design variable — how much the system says on someone's behalf.

  • Abstract Circles — abstraction. Presence as form and motion, nothing figurative.
  • Moving Shadows — degradation. Enough silhouette to read as a person, not enough to identify one.
  • Metaphorical Bees — metaphor. Activity carried by a swarm.

Ambiguity here is the design lever, not a limitation. Say too much and the display becomes surveillance. Say too little and it carries nothing anyone can use. The three concepts sit at different points on that trade-off so it could be tested rather than argued about.

Choosing between them

A comparative lab study with 24 participants, all three displays running in a simulated office. Fixed scenarios, Likert measures, semi-structured interviews.

The measurement instrument was itself a design problem. A paper questionnaire flattens exactly the kind of experience I was asking about — people tick a box and stop thinking. So I built a physical five-point scale participants moved with their hands, and used the object as the spine of the interview. People reason out loud while their hands are busy, and the position they settle on becomes something to discuss rather than a number to record.

The study showed the designs supported social presence and situational awareness. Published at ACM CSCW 2025.

Then into real workplaces

A lab result on three displays is not a product. I built the full system — two screens, four sensor prototypes and the visualization software connecting them — and deployed it with five teams, twenty people, in their own workplaces, for weeks.

I arranged access with each team, maintained the hardware on site, and kept people engaged for the duration. Questionnaires, interviews and workshops ran together so the three sources could be read against each other rather than in isolation.

Across the studies, two qualities did the work. Interpretive flexibility: people read the display through their own relationships rather than against a fixed meaning, which is what let the same design sit comfortably in teams that worked very differently. And bounded accountability: someone could account for being away, or for not responding, without it turning into an obligation. Awareness opened space for an exchange instead of demanding one.

What I owned

Discovery interviews with knowledge workers across several countries. The framework. Three concept designs and their visual design. The sensor hardware. The software — HTML, CSS and JavaScript, Python, Raspberry Pi. Deployment logistics and on-site maintenance. Both evaluations and all of the analysis. There was no one to hand any part of it to, which is the reason I know what each part costs.

What I would do differently

The work sits inside one academic organisation and covers short, arranged use rather than months of ordinary work. I know how the displays land in week one; I do not know how they settle in month six, and that is the question a product team would ask first.

The ambiguity that makes these designs work also lets them be misread, and the system offers nothing to correct a misreading. That trade-off is acceptable where relational ease matters more than precise coordination, and probably not acceptable where it doesn't. Making that call is a product decision, not a research one — and it depends on whose work the system is describing.
